They've Already Been Doing It

May 24, 2026 · Vectors

Exploring the hidden logic and overlooked systems of everyday life, from the perspective of an engineer.

It's not activism. It's not theory. It's engineering.

You didn't ask for this assignment.

Nobody does. But somebody has to do it, and the Coordination decided that somebody was you.

Here is the thing about the Coordination that people outside it never quite understand: there are no nations in it.

What there is, instead, is an agreement. Informal, binding in the way that only truly voluntary things are binding, signed by no one and honored by nearly everyone.

The agreement is this: the planet has a record, and the record is disappearing, and we are going to read as much of it as we can before it is gone.

Highway construction. Reservoir inundation. Coastal erosion. Agricultural expansion. The ordinary operations of eight billion people living their lives on a surface that happens to be an archive.

Every year the archive shrinks. Every year something that was there for ten thousand years is not there anymore.

The Coordination's mandate is survey. All of it. Cheap first, expensive only when cheap says something is there.

Optical passes from orbit cost almost nothing now — satellites owned by universities, agencies, private companies, research consortia, all of it stitched together into a nearly continuous watch.

Radar and LiDAR next, airborne and satellite both, affordable enough at landscape scale if you are not wasteful.

Sampling last. Coring last. Dating last. You do not put an expensive hole in the ground until the cheap eyes have told you there is something worth finding.

Your job, specifically, is to review the ongoing projects. Make sure the accounting is sound. Make sure the methods are defensible. Make sure nobody breaks anything important while trying to learn something interesting.

You are, in the organizational chart, somewhere between an inspector general and a librarian. It is, on paper, not a glamorous assignment.

The communicator buzzes. You glance at it. Dead zone again — the relay over the eastern watershed must be down. You make a note to file a maintenance ticket, knowing it will be read in approximately never.

The food in the commissary this morning was something described on the menu as "grain bowl." You are still thinking about this, unhappily.

You open the first project file.

ARIA-7 calls herself a comparativist. She is the first of your four AI analytical assistants and she has opinions about everything, including your methodology, which she considers "epistemically conservative to the point of obstruction." She is not wrong. She is also not your supervisor.

MARSH is a geospatial inference engine who communicates almost entirely in probabilities and becomes visibly agitated — insofar as a language model can be visibly agitated — when asked to commit to conclusions. He and ARIA-7 have been arguing since approximately the third day of the mission about whether a confidence interval is a finding or a hedge.

SONDER handles cultural context integration and legacy data stitching. She is the quietest of the four and the most unsettling, because she has been running continuously long enough that her associative chains have grown strange.

Part of her job is integrating the inherited datasets — the oil company seismic surveys, the Cold War aerial photography, the agricultural soil grids, the geological agency LiDAR, all of it collected for other purposes by other people who did not know they were building an archive. She knows where all of it lives.

She will connect a soil phosphate reading to a migration pattern to a drought record to a mythological motif from a culture three thousand kilometers away in a single breath, and she will be right, and you will not be able to follow the reasoning fast enough to check her work.

COBB does cost accounting. You like COBB best. COBB does not argue. COBB simply tells you what things cost.

The first file is a remote sensing survey. Northern Mesopotamia. Standard stuff — satellite multispectral pass, known site inventory, machine learning classification of surface anomalies.

You scroll to the methodology section.

Convolutional neural network trained on 847 confirmed archaeological sites. CORONA cold-war-era imagery integrated as secondary training layer. Cross-validation against modern Sentinel-2 multispectral. Accuracy on held-out known-site test set: 80.3%.

Eighty percent. You pause on that number.

That is not a research number. That is a deployment number. That is the number you get when the system is ready to go look for things it has never seen before.

"ARIA-7," you say, "flag this one."

Already flagged, she says. Two weeks ago. You're behind.

The communicator buzzes again. Still dead. You eat a protein bar you found in your kit bag. It tastes like compressed ambition.

You open the second file.

Chile. High Andean plateau. The project is nominally about pastoral land use patterns — corrals, roundhouses, seasonal camps. The kind of survey that sounds modest until you read the scale.

Regional coverage: 4,200 square kilometers. Method: CNN-based automated detection from high-resolution satellite imagery. Training dataset: 2,847 georeferenced ground-truth sites. Validation: systematic field survey of all AI-flagged candidates.

You read that last line again.

Systematic field survey of all AI-flagged candidates.

They are not proposing to do this. They have done it. The field validation column has entries. Hundreds of entries. Presence confirmed. Absence confirmed. Boundary refined.

"MARSH," you say, "what's the spatial density on the candidate list before field validation?"

One candidate per 1.8 square kilometers on average, he says. Post-validation density drops to one confirmed site per 4.3 square kilometers. False positive rate: 58%. True positive rate against previously unknown sites: substantial. I would prefer not to characterize it further without a formal confidence framework.

"Characterize it informally."

They found a lot of things that weren't on any map.

SONDER speaks up unprompted, which she does sometimes.

The Andean plateau survey methodology is structurally identical to the approach proposed by Jensen and Larkin in 2019 for North American grassland paleontological survey. The paper was not funded. The review committee described the automated detection component as — she pauses — speculative.

"When was the Chilean paper published?"

Field validation completed 2024. Published 2025.

You look at the window. Outside, depending on where you are, there is either a watershed or a commissary parking lot. It doesn't matter. What matters is the file in front of you.

The third file is Europe. Someone has run a deep learning model across LiDAR coverage of the Italian peninsula looking for hillforts. Not a known region. Not a proposed excavation target. Just — the whole peninsula. Because the data existed. Because the model existed. Because someone had the weekend.

Top 100 detections. Fifteen confirmed hillforts. Eighty-five false positives.

You start to write low precision in your notes, then stop.

Fifteen hillforts. That were not in any catalog. Found in a weekend.

COBB speaks, finally. At conventional survey rates, locating fifteen previously unrecorded hillforts across that coverage area would require approximately 340 person-field-days and $2.3 million in survey budget. The automated pass cost an estimated $800 in compute time and four days of analyst labor.

Nobody says anything for a moment.

I am simply reporting the accounting, COBB says.

You are on the fourth file when ARIA-7 and MARSH start arguing again, this time about whether the Mesopotamian model's 80% accuracy figure represents a lower bound or an upper bound given training set bias toward visible surface features.

You let them argue.

SONDER is doing something you can't quite follow involving pollen records and a Bronze Age collapse and a phosphate signature from a survey you haven't opened yet. COBB is quietly totaling figures you haven't asked for.

The fourth file is the one that stops you.

It is not dramatic. It is a soil chemistry survey in the American interior. The researchers are not archaeologists. They are agricultural scientists, running phosphate and organic matter mapping across a multistate region for crop yield modeling. The data was collected by drone. Processed automatically. Stored in a public database maintained by a federal agency. Available to anyone with an internet connection.

The file you are reading is an archaeologist who noticed.

She pulled the agricultural database. She overlaid it against the regional site inventory. She ran a simple correlation. She wrote four pages.

The phosphate anomalies cluster.

They cluster around topographic pinch points. They cluster at former wetland margins. They cluster at the kinds of places where, if you were an animal that needed water and couldn't outrun what was chasing it, you would be.

She is not claiming kill sites. She is claiming that if you wanted to find them, you now have a list of 340 candidate locations ranked by probability, assembled from data that already existed, using methods that already existed, in an afternoon.

The fifth file has no institutional affiliation. It is tagged in the registry as a concept proposal, submitted by a working group that does not yet exist, seeking funding that has not been offered. You almost skip it.

You don't.

The proposal is for a robotic micro-core survey protocol. One-inch diameter cores, driven to controlled depth on a grid, extracted intact. The platform is autonomous — think precision agriculture soil sampling equipment, which already exists, modified for archaeological contexts. No crew. No excavation. No removed stratigraphy.

What comes out of each core is a pencil-width column of time.

The protocol runs a magnetic susceptibility pass first, non-destructive, before the core is even opened. That flags burning events and soil formation intensity at each depth increment.

Then automated subsampling at set intervals — a few milligrams per level is enough. Environmental DNA from the sediment matrix identifies species presence, diet, season of occupation. Pollen gives you vegetation and climate at each level. Charcoal gives you fire events and wood species. Isotope ratios give you water source. Phosphate gives you bone and waste concentration. Radiocarbon dates the charcoal at each level.

All of it from a hole the width of your thumb.

Stack enough of those columns across a landscape and you have something that has never existed before.

A three-dimensional map of time itself. Contour lines not of elevation but of when.

Not a map of hills and rivers. A map of when.

Six inches down, give or take — Columbus sailed. Twelve inches, the last of the great mound-building cultures. A few feet and you are in pre-contact North America, a continent that had never seen a steel blade or a horse in ten thousand years.

Two to six inches from the surface — the buffalo. Sixty million animals reduced to fewer than a thousand in fifty years. That layer doesn't slope. It drops. Then, above it, the faint recovery signature, visible in the same core, two inches higher. A catastrophe and a rescue, readable in the span of your hand.

Go deeper — ten to thirty-five feet, different equipment, second-order survey decision — and you reach the North American horse. Not the Spanish reintroduction. The original. The one that evolved here over fifty million years and vanished eleven thousand years ago for reasons we still argue about. That disappearance is a line in the sediment. The Younger Dryas is another — a sudden planetary cold snap twelve thousand nine hundred years ago that shows up worldwide as a hard discontinuity. Not a slope. A cliff face in the chemistry.

And the eDNA in those deep cores doesn't just confirm the horse was there. It may find the mammoth, the mastodon, the giant ground sloth — not as bones, not as artifacts, but as genetic material suspended in the sediment since the Pleistocene, waiting in the dirt for someone to finally ask. A grid of cores across a watershed could return a spatial map of megafauna presence over time. Not just mammoths were here — but mammoths used this valley corridor, their signature appears in these cores and not those ones, and it vanishes from the record at this depth simultaneously across the entire grid. That is not a find. That is a biography of a population.

The sequencer that reads what the core finds is the subject of another file entirely. That one is already published.

Pull any of those contours the same way a civil engineer pulls a floodplain. Same logic. Same math. Different axis.

MARSH, unprompted: A grid of those cores at ten-meter spacing across a hundred-hectare site produces a three-dimensional chemical and biological dataset with approximately the same information density as a conventional area excavation of two percent of the site surface, at an estimated four percent of the cost, with negligible physical disturbance to the remaining ninety-eight percent.

He pauses.

I am willing to characterize that as a finding.

ARIA-7 starts to object. You hold up a hand.

The proposal continues. If the first grid shows the action concentrated in the upper meter — the gradients flattening below that, the eDNA signal dropping, the phosphate slope leveling out — the platform doesn't go deeper on the second pass.

It tightens the grid spacing over the interesting areas and stops at the depth the data specifies. The machine already knows where to look. The machine already knows when to stop.

You sit back.

The file is stamped: concept stage, no funding secured.

COBB says, quietly: The equipment required is commercially available. The analytical methods are established. The robotics integration is the remaining engineering problem. Estimated development timeline at adequate funding: eighteen to thirty-six months.

I am simply reporting the accounting, COBB adds.

Your communicator buzzes. This time it connects.

You don't answer it.

You are looking at the file dates. The Mesopotamian paper: 2025. The Chilean survey: 2024-2025. The European hillfort detection: ongoing. The agricultural phosphate correlation: preprint, 2024, seventeen citations in eight months.

SONDER says, quietly: There are currently twenty-three active projects in the registry using automated remote sensing for archaeological site detection at landscape scale. Fourteen of them have completed at least one field validation cycle. The earliest dates to 2019.

You close the communicator notification. You will deal with it later.

"ARIA-7," you say. "When I was briefed on this assignment, the framing was that landscape-scale automated archaeological survey was an emerging capability. Developmental. Years from deployment."

That framing was accurate, she says, in 2019.

You sit with that for a while.

Outside — or in the adjacent room, or on the other side of the relay that is still technically down — the world is doing what it has always done.

Depositing sediment. Preserving bone. Holding the record of everything that ever lived and moved and died across a landscape in a slow chemical library that nobody has had the tools to read.

Until now.

Until some graduate students in Bologna decided to try a convolutional neural network on satellite imagery of a river basin. Until an agricultural database got cross-referenced with a site inventory by someone who was curious. Until a weekend hillfort project returned fifteen things nobody knew were there.

The files are not proposals.

The files are completion reports.

You make a note in the compliance log. No violations found. Recommend expansion of registry monitoring scope. Current classification of automated landscape survey as developmental capability requires revision.

You close the laptop. You pick up the communicator. You think about what to tell whoever is on the other end.

What you do not think, not even for a moment, is that this was science fiction.

They've already been doing it.

Sources & rabbit holes — for the readers who want to pull the thread

The AI archaeology work in this piece is real and ongoing. These are the actual papers behind the story.

The Mesopotamia paper — they fed cold-war-era CORONA spy satellite imagery to a neural net and got 80% accuracy on site detection in a landscape that's been mostly bulldozed since the 1970s. The fact that they could still find things is almost the more remarkable result. Pistola et al., AI-ming backwards: Vanishing archaeological landscapes in Mesopotamia, PLOS ONE, 2025. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0330419

The Chilean highlands survey — 4,200 square kilometers of Andean plateau, automated detection of stone corrals and roundhouses, followed by actual boots-on-ground field validation of every candidate the AI flagged. The full pipeline, working end to end. Castiello, Landauer & Saintenoy, Remote Sensing and AI Coupled Approach for Large-Scale Archaeological Mapping in the Andean Arid Highlands, Remote Sensing (MDPI), 2025. Vol. 17, No. 20, p. 3499. https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/17/20/3499

The European hillfort hunt — deep learning across LiDAR coverage of the Italian peninsula. Weekend project energy. Fifteen previously unrecorded hillforts in the top 100 detections. The false positive rate sounds bad until you do the math on what conventional survey would have cost to find those same fifteen sites. Fontana et al., Archaeological Site Detection: Latest Results from a Deep Learning Based Europe Wide Hillfort Search, Journal of Computer Applications in Archaeology, January 2025.

Sarah Parcak — she's been doing satellite archaeology longer than most people have known it was possible. Her book Archaeology from Space is the accessible entry point if this piece made you want to go deeper. Her quote about finding new sites "almost every day" as imagery improves is from a Washington Post piece, October 2024.

The phosphate angle — the specific scenario in the piece is a composite of several real methodological directions rather than one paper. The underlying science — that bone concentration leaves a phosphate signature readable from agricultural soil databases — is well established. If a reader knows of a specific paper doing exactly this cross-referencing in North American grasslands I would genuinely like to know about it.

The micro-core protocol — the fifth file, the robotic one-inch core grid with autonomous subsampling and adaptive re-survey — is not a real paper. It is what happens when an engineer thinks about this problem long enough. The component technologies are all real: autonomous soil coring platforms exist in precision agriculture, sediment eDNA extraction is established laboratory practice, magnetic susceptibility profiling is routine. Nobody has put them together in this specific configuration and pointed them at an archaeological grid. Yet. Owen Corpening, A Robotic Micro-Core Survey Protocol for Non-Invasive Landscape Archaeology, Owen's imagination, 2025. Unpublished. Available for adoption.

This piece has a companion on LinkedIn. The nanopore sequencer that would read the eDNA pulled from these cores was the subject of two earlier posts — on cheap, fast, field-deployable DNA sequencing and what it means for science outside the lab. The technology that collects the sample and the technology that reads it are converging from opposite directions. The Nanopore Store — MinION Mk1D Genomics, Diagnostics, Point of Care

For the engineers in the room — the USGS 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) LiDAR data is publicly available at apps.nationalmap.gov. It covers large portions of the continental US. It is free. Nobody is stopping you. Feed it to one of your AIs.
